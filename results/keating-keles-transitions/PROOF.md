# The transition forms

Let the order be $n\ge1$, the upper entries range over $[0,t]$, and the fixed subdiagonal be $s$, with $s,t>0$. Divide the matrix by $t$ and write $r=s/t$, $h=1/r$, $m=n-1$. Multiply each normalized determinant bound below by $t^n$ to undo the normalization.

## Maximum and equality

Use $m$ updates from $(1,0)$. Each update raises one coordinate by $h$ times the other. Write a word by the lengths of its consecutive runs, starting with an update of the second coordinate. If a run word ends at $(p,q)$, its normalized determinant magnitude is $r^m$ times the coordinate raised by its last update. In the following lists, $1^j$ means $j$ runs of length one. For $m\ge2$ these are **all** the maximizing words.

* Let $r$ be nonintegral and $k=\lceil r\rceil$. If $m>2k$, the only maximizing word is $(k,1^{m-2k},k)$. If $m\le2k$, put $u=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$, $v=\lceil m/2\rceil$. The balanced two-run words $(u,v),(v,u)$ give $T=1+uvh^2$. The single run $(m)$ gives $S=mh$. Take exactly those types giving the larger of $S,T$. Equivalently the single run wins precisely when $u<r<v$.
* Let $r=d$ be a positive integer. If $m\ge2d+2$, take all $(a,1^{m-a-b},b)$ with $a,b\in\{d,d+1\}$ and $m-a-b\ge0$. A zero exponent gives two runs. If $m=2d+1$, take $(d,1,d),(m),(d,d+1),(d+1,d)$. If $m=2d$ or $m=2d-1$, take $(m)$ and both balanced two-run words, identifying duplicates. If $m<2d-1$, take only the balanced two-run words.

To obtain the matrix, color vertices $0,\ldots,n$. Vertex zero has color $+$. Internal vertex $i$ has the color of the coordinate raised at step $i$, with the second coordinate colored $-$. Give vertex $n$ the opposite color to vertex $n-1$. At $(i,j)$ on the upper triangle enter one exactly if $i$ and $j+1$ have different colors, and zero otherwise. The subdiagonal is $r$.

There is also a single recurrence for the numerical answer. Start with $(a,b)=(1,0)$. Repeat $\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ times: replace $(a,b)$ by the decreasing rearrangement of $(a,b+ha)$. For even $m$ the normalized maximum is $r^m(a^2+b^2)$. For odd $m$ it is
$$r^m\max\{a^2+b^2+hab,\;2ab+ha^2\}.$$
This includes $n=1,2$. At $n=1$ only the entry one attains the maximum. At $n=2$ write the determinant as $ad-rb$. The positive extremum attains the absolute maximum exactly when $r\le1$, $a=d=1,b=0$. The negative extremum does so exactly when $r\ge1$, $b=1,ad=0$, allowing a continuous family.

## Reduction to words and rigidity of the matrix

Set $x_0=1$ and $x_{j+1}=-h\sum_{i\le j} A_{ij}x_i$. Adding $x_i$ times row $i$ to row zero for $1\le i<n$ kills the first $n-1$ columns in that row. Expansion in the last column gives $\det A=(-r)^n x_n$. If $p,q$ are the sums of the positive and negative parts of $x_0,\dots,x_j$, then $-hp\le x_{j+1}\le hq$. Replacing each step by the extreme of its sign yields a dominating word starting at $(1,0)$. Conversely every word and either terminal coordinate are realized by the color construction, choosing the final color opposite the chosen coordinate. Thus the maximum divided by $r^m$ is the largest accessible coordinate after $m$ shears.

Write this word maximum as $M_m$. The first step raising the second coordinate dominates the other choice, so there is an optimizer with both coordinates positive after step one. Extending such an optimizer by raising its larger coordinate proves $M_{m+1}>M_m$ for $m\ge1$. (The first step can be replaced even when the original optimizer had zero second coordinate.) For $m\ge2$ a maximizing word therefore has a productive first step and observes the last coordinate it raised. All intermediate coefficients in the saturated word are nonzero. A deficit at any step strictly decreases the terminal output: a deficit in its observed coordinate persists, and a deficit in its other coordinate enters the observed coordinate by the last shear. A deficit at the final coefficient also decreases the determinant. Hence equality forces saturation at every step. With all earlier coefficients nonzero, attaining the appropriate extremum of a weighted sum forces every upper entry individually to equal the prescribed zero or one. This proves rigidity over the real box.

## The exchange argument

Write $L(x,y)=(x+hy,y)$ and $R(x,y)=(x,y+hx)$. Suppose $a>b\ge0$. Compare raising the larger coordinate and then the smaller-coordinate *index* (first $L$, then $R$) with the opposite ordering. The respective results are
$$v=(a+hb,b+ha+h^2b),\qquad w=(a+hb+h^2a,b+ha).$$
The sum increases by $h^2(a-b)>0$. The first coordinate of $w$ exceeds both coordinates of $v$, by $h^2a>0$ and $(1-h+h^2)(a-b)>0$. For any nonnegative weights $c\ge d$ with $c>0$, the best alignment scores $(c-d)\max(x,y)+d(x+y)$, which strictly increases. Any suffix and terminal coordinate give such weights after sorting, and exchanging the two coordinates of the entire suffix and terminal choice is allowed. Thus a switch immediately after increasing a strictly larger coordinate precludes optimality. The same argument applies to a bilinear objective with final vector fixed up to coordinate exchange.

Within a run the raised coordinate never decreases. It follows that in a maximizing word every step outside its last run raises a weakly smaller coordinate. Applying transposition and reading backwards gives the corresponding requirement from the other end. Indeed $L^{\mathsf T}=R$ and $R^{\mathsf T}=L$, and either terminal coordinate is allowed. A global exchange of coordinates restores the starting vector $(1,0)$, retaining the run lengths in reverse order.

A word with at least three runs must cross equality at the end of its first run: its last update started weakly below the other coordinate, and the next update must also start weakly below the other coordinate. Its length $a$ therefore satisfies $(a-1)h\le1\le ah$. Immediately after crossing, the larger coordinate is at most $(1+h)$ times the smaller. From any positive sorted pair $A\ge B$ with $A\le(1+h)B$, raising $B$ gives $B+hA>A$, since $1/(1+h)+h>1$, and the same ratio bound holds afterwards. Equality at the initial crossing permits either color, accounting for the choices of first run. Consequently every intervening run has length one, and the last run satisfies the identical two bounds by reversal. In the two-run case, the necessary upper bound $(a-1)h\le1$ still holds at both ends. Its attained score is $1+abh^2$, largest at balanced lengths. The one-run score, when the raised coordinate is observed, is $mh$.

For nonintegral $r$, the two endpoint inequalities in a word of three or more runs force both ends to have length $k$. When $m>2k$, a two-run word cannot win by its upper endpoint bounds. A balanced two-run word already beats a single run since $T-S=(1-uh)(1-vh)>0$ there. Hence the displayed middle-alternating form must win. For $m\le2k$, a word with at least three runs cannot win, leaving exactly the displayed comparison.

For $r=d$, at length $d$ the two coordinates coincide. Flipping every subsequent coordinate choice and the observed coordinate preserves the score. At the other end the same operation works after transposition. When $m\ge2d+2$ the two boundary choices at steps $d+1$ from the two ends are disjoint. They toggle each endpoint length between $d$ and $d+1$ in the displayed forms, preserving the value. Equivalently the forms with endpoint lengths $d,d$ and $d+1,d+1$ attain the maximum by one-sided limits from the adjoining open intervals, and the two mixed choices come from flipping just one end. The single run loses strictly to $T$, and the endpoint bounds exhaust all other possibilities. For $m=2d+1$ the three-run value is $2dh+d^2h^3$ and equals $S=T$. For smaller $m$ three runs are impossible, so the factored comparison proves the remaining cases.

## Derivation of the half-length recurrence

At both ends of a maximizing word of length at least two consider the common starting vector up to exchange, now any sorted $(a,b)$ with $a> b\ge0$. An initial update to the larger coordinate forces a constant word by the exchange argument. Its best alignment scores $a^2+b^2+mhab$. Changing its first update to the other direction with the aligned endpoint increases the score by $(m-1)h^2a^2>0$. Thus both the first update and the transpose of the last must increase the smaller coordinate. If $a=b$, either gives the same sorted pair. Removing these two updates leaves identical new endpoints up to exchange, with either relative orientation attainable by choice of final orientation and transposed last update. Conversely any middle word with these endpoints extends to an allowed word of the original length. Iterating gives the stated formula: the empty middle has best dot product $a^2+b^2$, and the single middle shear has the two alignment scores in the formula.

## Consequences for the questions in §6

For $m=2u\ge2$, changes between the adjacent open regimes occur exactly at the integers $1,\ldots,u-1$. The isolated tie at $r=u$ adds one exceptional parameter without changing the adjacent regime. For $m=2u+1\ge3$, the changes occur at every integer $1,\ldots,u+1$, including the two ends of the single-run interval. These lists give both conventions for counting transitions. At $n=2$ the only change is at one; at $n=1$ there is none.

At $r=2$ the source matrix $U_n^{(rc)}$ attains the maximum for every $n\ge4$. Its upper pattern is the single run $(3)$ at order four, $(2,2)$ at order five, and $(2,1^{m-4},2)$ for $n\ge6$. The two row/column exchanges in (4.1) give these colors independently of the numerical subdiagonal: only the lower entries change when $r$ changes. The entrywise parity check in the [fixed-ratio formalization](../../lean/Conjectures/KeatingKeles/Attainment.lean) identifies the upper entries. These are precisely the maximizing patterns immediately above one and they all appear in the boundary list at two. The classification gives three maximizing matrices at order four, two at order five and four at every larger order.

The second change is at two for $n=4$ and every $n\ge6$. For $n=5$ the source's $U_n^{(rc)}$ is the balanced two-run form and remains maximizing forever, including its isolated tie at two. Thus the supremal $\epsilon(n)$ in Remark 4.4 is one except at $n=5$, where it is infinite, for the orders $n\ge4$ of that remark.

The infimum of nonnegative tail thresholds for which the value in Theorem 5.2 holds everywhere above threshold is $u-1$ for $m=2u\ge2$ and $u+1$ for $m=2u+1\ge3$. Both include equality when the endpoint is positive. In the odd-$m$ case the formula also holds at $r=u$; the intervening open interval requires the larger single-run value. The threshold grows as $n/2$. For orders one and two direct evaluation gives tail thresholds zero and one respectively; the term with zero coefficient in Theorem 5.2 is interpreted as zero.

Question 7 has a negative answer even with $n,r$ both fixed. At $n=5,r=2$, the words $(4)$ and $(2,2)$ both give determinant magnitude $32$. Their colors split the six vertices as $2+4$ and $3+3$, so they have eight and nine upper ones respectively. Question 8 has an affirmative answer throughout the positive-ratio domain. Each nonzero permutation of a Hessenberg matrix splits uniquely into consecutive blocks: in each block $[i,j]$ row $i$ uses column $j$ and rows $i+1,\ldots,j$ use the preceding column. To see this, start with the column chosen by row zero; all earlier columns are then successively forced to use their subdiagonal, and induction applies to the remainder. Blocks correspond to the edges of a path from $0$ to $n$, where each edge must switch color. If there are $e$ blocks, the permutation sign is $(-1)^{n-e}$. Endpoint colors fix the parity of $e$, so every nonzero term has the same sign. The directly classified small orders have the same property.

For the broader reading of Question 8 allowing nonpositive $s$ in Problem 2.4, the block argument also settles the sign question directly. At $s=0$ only the identity permutation can contribute. For $s<0$ a term with $e$ blocks uses $n-e$ negative subdiagonal entries. Its sign from entries cancels its permutation sign, so every nonzero signed term is positive. This last observation refers to the signs of the signed summands. If one interprets the question as parity of the underlying permutations, at $s=-1,t=1,n=2$ the all-ones upper triangle maximizes and both parities occur. The two readings coincide in the positive-ratio theorem.
